Path Bed

Full sun, dry

North-west corner, out by the street

Watered by Hose, by hand

North-west corner, out by the street and clear of the house. Being simplified: the sage and rosemary are coming out, the tulips are lifted, and the bearded iris is going too, which leaves shasta daisies and black-eyed Susans with the bed to themselves. Full sun and sharp drainage, which is what the herbs were thriving on, so the ground where they stood wants compost worked through it before the black-eyed Susans go in.

The plan Two plants, one long summer, and almost nothing to do between times.
  1. Lift the bearded iris while the window is open
    Now, it closes in early September

    Removal itself can happen any time, but this is the last week the rhizomes come up as firm divisions rather than a clump for the bin. Cut the leaves to a six inch fan, snap the rhizomes apart, keep what is firm and pass it on. Clear every old leaf off the ground while you are there, because iris leaf spot overwinters on dead foliage and this bed is about to be replanted.

  2. Dig the herb stumps out after the first proper rain
    October

    Not in August. A woody root ball comes out of damp ground with a fraction of the effort, and nothing suffers by waiting. Get the roots out rather than leaving them to rot in place, since that is exactly where the new planting goes.

  3. Work compost into where the herbs stood
    Autumn, before planting

    That corner grew Mediterranean herbs for years, which want it lean and dry, and black-eyed Susans want more moisture than that. A couple of buckets forked through the top spit. With the iris gone there is nothing left here that rots under compost, so the improving and the planting no longer pull against each other.

  4. Plant the black-eyed Susans
    Autumn if you can get plants, spring otherwise

    Autumn is the better window here: warm soil, the rains arrive to do the establishing, and you barely water. Buy the clumping perennial kinds such as 'Goldsturm'. Do not feed beyond the compost. Fed rudbeckia flops, and so does shasta daisy, and this bed is exposed.

  5. Divide both, on the same clock
    Early spring, about every three years

    This is the whole maintenance of the bed and the reason the pairing is worth having. Both want dividing on roughly the same cycle, both want it in early spring rather than autumn, and both want an inch of compost and nothing else. One date, one job, the entire bed.

Watch for
  • Nothing flowers here between October and June. That is the real cost of simplifying, and it lands in the bed most visible from the street. Daffodils planted under the perennials would cover March and April, ask nothing after planting, and already return and multiply in the front beds. Not planted, and not decided.
  • Flopping, with a dead centre and the flowers pushed out to the edges, is the shasta daisy asking to be divided. That is the three year clock telling you it is due.
  • Angular brown or black spots climbing up the lower leaves of the black-eyed Susan is Septoria leaf spot, which rudbeckia get badly in this climate. Take the affected leaves off, water the soil rather than the foliage, and clear every dead leaf in autumn.

Driveway Bed 1

Full sun

North-east corner, beyond the driveway

Watered by Hose, by hand

North-east corner beyond the driveway, out by the street and clear of the house. The annual patch: sunflowers, zinnias and a wildflower mix, grown here on purpose and staying that way. All three want full sun, which is the evidence for the sun rating.

The plan Stout single stems and big heads, not a thicket of thin ones.
  1. Keep sowing in fall
    October into November

    This already works here and it is the reason the patch comes up strong, so it stays. The seed sits through winter and germinates the week the soil is ready, which is earlier than you would dare sow by hand, and the taproot is never disturbed. Anything started in a pot in May is behind before it goes in.

  2. Get the seedlings past the slugs
    The first three weeks after they emerge

    This is where a fall-sown patch is won or lost. The seedlings come up into a wet spring, which is peak slug season, and a sunflower at two leaves is one night's work. Check every couple of days from first emergence until they are hand height. After that they are too tough to matter.

  3. Thin, and harder than feels reasonable
    At four to six inches tall

    This is the whole answer to scraggly. Volunteers come up in clumps where the heads fell, and ten seedlings in a hand's width gives you ten thin whippy stems with small heads, every time. Thin to eighteen inches apart for the single-stem types and twelve for the branching one. Cut the extras off at soil level instead of pulling, so you do not tear the roots of the one you are keeping.

  4. Water deep, and not often
    July and August

    Deep and infrequent drives the taproot down, and a deep taproot is what makes a tall sunflower stand up by itself in an exposed spot out by the street. A daily splash keeps the roots near the surface and gives you exactly the leaning you are trying to avoid. One long slow soak a week beats a short one every day.

  5. Earth up the tall ones
    Once they are knee high

    Draw soil up around the base of the big single-stem plants. It anchors them, and it is far quicker than staking a whole patch.

Watch for
  • Thin stems with small heads. In a bed this sunny that is crowding, not light, and it means the thinning did not happen or did not go far enough.
  • Powdery mildew, which crowding causes too by killing air movement. Thinning fixes the mildew and the scraggly stems in one job.
  • The branching yellow will never be one tall stout stalk. That is what a branching variety is: many smaller flowers on thinner stems, by design. If tall and strong is the goal, save seed from the mahogany, and buy a single-stem mammoth type to sow alongside it.

Shake the poppy seed where you want it

Those hundreds of little pods are ripe or nearly ripe, and once they shatter the seed goes wherever it goes. Hold each stem over the spot you want poppies next spring and tip the pod. It pours out like a pepper shaker.

Shirley poppies will not transplant and resent spring sowing, so this fall drop is your only real shot at next year's display. Put a pinch in an envelope as insurance before you scatter the rest.

If you skip it The pods shatter and next spring's poppies come up wherever they happen to land, not where you want them.

Street Bed Box 1Street Bed Box 2

Deadhead the gaillardia hard, then the dahlias

Across the photos there are roughly two dozen spent seed globes for every open gaillardia flower. Once the plant decides it has made enough seed, it quits blooming. Cut each spent globe back to a leaf or side shoot, not just the ball off the top.

Same move on the dahlias: cut back to a set of leaves or a stem junction. Buds are round and firm, spent heads are pointed and conical. Then the scabiosa. Done properly this is worth six to eight more weeks of flowers.

If you skip it Nothing dies. The plants set seed and stop flowering several weeks earlier than they would have.

Wait for the first proper rain rather than fighting bone-dry August soil. A woody root ball comes out of damp ground with a fraction of the effort.

Lift the tulip bulbs out of Path Bed

This is the right moment and nearly nobody realises it. Tulips are dormant from about July through October, and dormant is exactly when you can lift and move them. Do it at the same time as you dig out the sage and rosemary, since you are in that corner anyway.

The awkward part is finding them, because there is no foliage marking the spot. Work with a fork rather than a spade so you spear fewer of them, start well outside where you think they are, and lift wide and deep, sifting the soil as you go.

Sort as you lift. Firm and heavy is good. Soft, light, papery or carrying blue-green mould goes in the bin. Then either replant straight away in their new spot, or store them somewhere cool, dark and dry in paper bags or mesh, never sealed plastic, and plant with the rest of your bulbs in October or November.

If you skip it Once they sprout you cannot move them without wrecking the year, and the next chance is after the foliage dies back in June. That is a full season lost, and they will be in the way of the new planting the whole time.

Street Bed Box 1

Pull the rusted snapdragons out, do not just cut them

This reverses what I told you earlier. I said to cut the spent spikes and leave the plants standing, because snapdragons often overwinter here and return for a second year. That is right for a healthy plant and wrong for a rusted one: the fungus survives winter on living tissue and on debris underneath, so leaving them is how it comes back.

Pull the affected plants out roots and all, rake up the fallen leaves beneath them, and bag the lot for the household bin. Do not put it in the compost tumbler: a home heap rarely runs hot enough for long enough to kill rust spores.

The reassuring part is that snapdragon rust only attacks snapdragons. The gladiolus behind them, and everything else in that box, are not at risk from it.

Next spring, put snapdragons somewhere else rather than back in the same spot, look for varieties sold as rust-resistant, give them space so air moves between them, and water the soil rather than the leaves.

If you skip it The rust overwinters on the plants and the fallen leaves, and comes back into next year's snapdragons from the same spot. Nothing else in the bed is at risk, so it is not urgent in the way a spreading disease would be, it just resets the problem every year until you break it.

The highest-leverage three weeks of the gardening year, and almost nobody uses them. Hardy annuals sown now germinate in warm soil, overwinter as small plants, and bloom in May and June, months earlier and twice the size of anything spring-sown.

What to put on the beds

Compost and soil conditioner sit next to each other on the same shelf, cost about the same, and do completely different jobs. That is the single most useful thing to get straight.

Compost

Food and biology

Finished organic matter, dark and crumbly, no recognisable pieces left in it.

Use One to two inches spread on top of the bed once a year, in fall. Winter rain works it in without any digging. Keep it off crowns and off stems, and off the bearded iris rhizomes entirely, which need to sit exposed to the air.

Avoid Digging it into an established bed. You tear through roots to bury something that was going to get there anyway.

Covers A 1.5 cubic foot bag covers about 9 square feet at two inches deep, or 18 square feet at one inch.

This is the annual routine. If only one thing goes down, it is this.

Soil conditioner, or bark fines

Structure, not food

Finely ground bark or sawdust. It looks like compost in the bag and is sold beside it, which is why it gets bought by mistake.

Use Only where drainage is genuinely bad: ground that stands in water after rain, or soil that is dense and grey when you dig it. Spread it on the surface like compost.

Avoid Treating it as compost, and digging it in. It is almost pure carbon, so the microbes breaking it down pull nitrogen out of the soil to do the work, and a bed with it dug through is a hungry bed for a season. On the surface that reaction stays in the top inch and does not reach the roots.

Covers Same as compost by volume, but it should be a repair, not an annual habit.

A fix for a specific problem. Buy it when a bed drains badly, not every fall.

Bagged manure

Nitrogen

Steer or chicken, composted or not.

Use Spring, if at all, and only well composted.

Avoid Putting it down in fall here. The nitrogen washes through a Seattle winter before roots want it, and fresh manure burns.

Skip it. Compost does this job slowly and does not leach.

Lime

Raises pH

Ground limestone. Soils here are naturally acidic and winter rain makes them more so.

Use Only after a soil test says so.

Avoid Liming on principle. It is the one amendment worth testing for rather than guessing at, and it is actively wrong around anything acid loving.

Do nothing until you have a number.

Fall fertilizer

None worth having

Any balanced or high nitrogen feed applied in autumn.

Use Never in fall.

Avoid All of it. Nitrogen pushes soft new growth exactly when the plant should be shutting down, and that growth is what frost takes first. Most of it leaches out before spring anyway.

The amendment to leave in the shop.

Everything on this list arrives in bags, because there are no leaves in quantity here and no compost pile. Any fall leaves that can be raked up and left on the beds are the one free version of the first item on this list.

None.
Deadheading
Not individual flowers. Shear the entire plant back to a few inches once the main flush is over. It looks brutal, and within two or three weeks you have a fresh mound of clean foliage and often a second lot of flowers.
Doing well
A tidy dense mound.

How to divide itEarly spring, or straight after the summer shear.

How often Every three or four years

  1. Water the plant well the day before. A hydrated clump divides far better than a dry one.
  2. Cut any top growth back to a few inches so you can see what you are doing and the divisions are not fighting to support leaves.
  3. Dig a full circle around the clump, a spade's width outside the leaves, then lever underneath and lift the whole thing out.
  4. Shake or hose off enough soil to see the crown. You are looking for where the growth points sit.
  5. Split it. Two forks back to back in the middle and pushed apart works for tough clumps; a spade or a knife works for smaller ones. Each division needs both roots and at least two or three growth points.
  6. Throw away the old woody middle. It is the least productive part and the reason the clump was declining.

How to photograph a plant for ID

5 shots per plant

Most plant photos fail for the same reason: they are taken from standing height, in bright sun, showing the flower and nothing else. The flower is often the least diagnostic part. What settles an identification is usually how the leaves attach to the stem, and that is the shot almost nobody takes.

  1. The whole plant, from its own height

    Crouch down so the camera is level with the middle of the plant, and step back far enough to get all of it in frame.

    Why Habit is diagnostic. Upright and stiff, mounding, sprawling, climbing: these separate plants that look identical up close.

  2. Where a leaf meets the stem

    One clear shot of a stem joint. Get close enough that you can count the leaves at that joint.

    Why This single shot does more work than all the others. Two leaves facing each other is opposite, one at a time alternating is alternate, three or more radiating like spokes is whorled. It splits the plant world into thirds before anything else is considered.

  3. One leaf, top and underside

    Hold a leaf flat against a plain background, a sheet of paper or your palm. Take the top, then flip it and take the underside. Put a coin or your thumb in frame for scale.

    Why Undersides carry the detail: hairs, silvering, raised veins, and any pests. Scale matters because many close relatives differ mainly in size.

  4. The flower or seedhead, straight on

    Fill the frame. Tap the screen on the flower so the camera focuses there rather than on the background.

    Why Petal count, how the centre is arranged, and whether flowers sit singly or in clusters all narrow things down fast.

  5. The base, where it meets the soil

    Push the surrounding foliage aside and shoot the bottom few inches.

    Why Woody or soft, one crown or many spreading shoots, and whether it is running sideways. This is how you tell a perennial from an annual, and a well-behaved plant from a colonising one.

While you are out there

  • Shade the plant with your own body or a piece of card. Harsh direct sun blows out exactly the fine detail that identifies a plant. An overcast Seattle morning is genuinely ideal.
  • Hold a sheet of plain paper behind a leaf or flower to isolate it from the busy background.
  • Crush a leaf and note the smell in your own words. Anise, mint, resin, tomato, bitter, or nothing at all. Smell is not photographable and it eliminates whole families.
  • Roll the stem between your fingers and note whether it is round or square. Square means the mint family, which covers a lot of ground.
  • Say where it is in a voice memo or note as you shoot: bed, and roughly where in the bed. Four unknowns are easy to mix up later.

Things that beat taking more photos

Look back through your own camera roll

This is the highest-value thing on the list. Scroll back to May, June and July. Everything currently a brown seedhead was a flower then, and a flower is far easier to identify. A photo from bloom time will settle more of these than anything you can shoot today.

Find the seed packets or the order history

A wildflower or cutting mix packet lists every species in it. That converts guessing into matching against a known list. Check the shed, the drawer, and any online orders from late winter.

Stake the unknowns now

Push a labelled marker in beside each one, even just a numbered plant tag. Volunteers are often only identifiable in bloom, so the realistic plan is to tag them now and photograph them next spring. Without a marker you will not find the same plant again.

Take them to a Master Gardener clinic

WSU Extension runs free plant clinics in King County where trained volunteers identify samples in person. Bring a cut stem with leaves attached in a bag, not just a photo. An expert holding the actual plant beats any amount of remote guessing.

Accept that some wait for spring

A volunteer that has finished flowering and dropped its seed may genuinely not be identifiable until it comes back. That is not a failure, it is the season. Log where it is and revisit it in April.
